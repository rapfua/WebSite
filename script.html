Let us study the probability mass function of \(\mathcal H_n(U_a, U_b)\).

\[ \begin{align*} p_{\mathcal H_n(U_a, U_b)}(y) & = \mathrm{P} \left( \mathcal H_n(U_a, U_b) = y \right) \\ & = \sum_{\substack{ i , j \in [n] \\ v_i \in A_n, v_j \in B_n}} \mathrm{P} \left( \mathcal H_n(v_i, v_j) = y, U_a = v_i, U_b = v_j\right) \\ & = \sum_{\substack{ i, j \in [n] \\ v_i \in A_n, v_j \in B_n}} \mathrm P \left( \sum_{t \in \mathcal S} \mathbf 1_{E_t} \cdot \left( G_{\kappa_n, a_n}^{a,t, i} + G_{\kappa_n, C_n^{\text{con}}}^{b,t, j} \right) = y, U_a = v_i, U_b = v_j \right) \\ & \stackrel{\perp \!\! \perp}{=} \frac{1}{|A_n| \cdot |B_n|} \sum_{\substack{ i, j \in [n] \\ v_i \in A_n, v_j \in B_n}} \mathrm P \left( \sum_{t \in \mathcal S} \mathbf 1_{E_t} \cdot \left( G_{\kappa_n, a_n}^{a,t, i} + G_{\kappa_n, C_n^{\text{con}}}^{b,t, j} \right) = y \right) \\ & = \frac{1}{|A_n| \cdot |B_n|} \sum_{\substack{ i, j \in [n] \\ v_i \in A_n, v_j \in B_n}} \mathrm P \left( \sum_{t \in \mathcal S} \mathbf 1_{E_t} \cdot \left( G_{\kappa_n, a_n}^{a,t} + G_{\kappa_n, C_n^{\text{con}}}^{b,t} \right) = y \right) && \text{identically distributed} \\ & = \mathrm P \left( \sum_{t \in \mathcal S} \mathbf 1_{E_t} \cdot \left( G_{\kappa_n, a_n}^{a,t} + G_{\kappa_n, C_n^{\text{con}}}^{b,t} \right) = y \right) \\ & = \mathrm P \left( \mathcal H_n^{ab} = y \right) \end{align*} \]

Let us study the probability mass function of \(\mathcal H_n\) conditional on the type of the sources.

\[ \begin{align*} p_{\mathcal H_n | U_1 \in A_n, U_2 \in B_n}(y) & = \frac {\mathrm{P} \left( \mathcal H_n = y, U_1 \in A_n, U_2 \in B_n \right)} {\mathrm{P} \left( U_1 \in A_n, U_2 \in B_n \right)} \\ & = \frac{\left( |A_n| + |B_n| \right)^2}{|A_n| \cdot |B_n|} \sum_{\substack{ i, j \in [n] \\ v_i \in A_n, v_j \in B_n}} \mathrm{P} \left( \mathcal H_n = y, U_1 = v_i, U_2 = v_j \right) \\ & = \frac{\left( |A_n| + |B_n| \right)^2}{|A_n| \cdot |B_n|} \sum_{\substack{ i, j \in [n] \\ v_i \in A_n, v_j \in B_n}} \mathrm P \left( \sum_{t \in \mathcal S} \mathbf 1_{E_t} \cdot \left( G_{\kappa_n, a_n}^{a,t, i} + G_{\kappa_n, C_n^{\text{con}}}^{b,t,j} \right) = y, U_1 = v_i, U_2 = v_j \right) \\ & \stackrel{\perp \!\! \perp}{=} \frac{1}{|A_n| \cdot |B_n|} \sum_{\substack{ i , j \in [n] \\ v_i \in A_n, v_j \in B_n}} \mathrm P \left( \sum_{t \in \mathcal S} \mathbf 1_{E_t} \cdot \left( G_{\kappa_n, a_n}^{a,t, i} + G_{\kappa_n, C_n^{\text{con}}}^{b,t, j} \right) = y \right) \\ & = \frac{1}{|A_n| \cdot |B_n|} \sum_{\substack{ i , j \in [n] \\ v_i \in A_n, v_j \in B_n}} \mathrm P \left( \sum_{t \in \mathcal S} \mathbf 1_{E_t} \cdot \left( G_{\kappa_n, a_n}^{a,t} + G_{\kappa_n, C_n^{\text{con}}}^{b,t} \right) = y \right) && \text{identically distributed} \\ & = \mathrm{P} \left( \sum_{t \in \mathcal S} \mathbf 1_{E_t} \cdot \left( G_{\kappa_n, a_n}^{a,t} + G_{\kappa_n, C_n^{\text{con}}}^{b,t} \right) = y \right) \\ & = \mathrm{P} \left( \mathcal H_n^{ab} = y \right). \end{align*} \]

Let \((s, G) \sim \text{wSBM}(n, \pi, p, F)\) with \(p_{ab, n} = B_{ab} \rho_n\)

For now, we restrict ourselves to the exponential case. That is \(F_{ab} \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda_{ab})\).

The following matrix is then defined :

\[ T \, \colon = \left[ \Lambda \odot B \right] \text{diag}(\pi). \]

This leads to the definition of the following vector:

\[ \tau \, \colon = T \mathbf 1_k, \]

where \(k\) is the number of communities.

The proposition then tells us that:

\[ \frac{1}{\tau_{\max}} \leq \frac{n \rho_n}{\ln n} C(u, v) \leq \frac{1}{\tau_{\min}} \tag{5}\]

To help understand the vector \(\tau\) intuitively, let us make its relationship with the expected degree vector \(d\), explicit.

Denote the minimum (respectively maximum) expected degree by \(d_{\min}\) (respectively \(d_{\max}\). If \(\lambda_{ab} = \lambda \; \forall a, b\), we then have:

\[ d_{\min} = \frac{\tau_{\min} \cdot n \rho_n}{\lambda} \quad \& \quad d_{\max} = \frac{\tau_{\max} \cdot n \rho_n}{\lambda}. \]

Recall that \(\rho_n = \frac{\ln n}{n} \; \& \; \lambda = 1\) in our ideal framework, so that the equation above becomes:

\[ d_{\min} = \tau_{\min} \cdot \ln n \quad \& \quad d_{\max} = \tau_{\max} \cdot \ln n. \]

Actually, we even have

\[ d = \ln n \cdot \tau, \] since \[ \tau_a = \sum_b B_{ab} \pi_b \quad \& \quad d_a = n \sum_b p_{ab} \pi_b. \]

Setting \(\rho_n\) as in (Equation 3), (Equation 5) simply becomes:

\[ \frac{1}{\tau_{\max}} \leq C(u, v) = \mathcal P_n (u, v) \leq \frac{1}{\tau_{\min}}. \tag{6}\]

Note that (Dreveton et al. 2024)’s \(\pi\) corresponds to (Kolossváry and Komjáthy 2015)’s \(\mu\).

Proof using Gumbel

  • Let \(\Lambda_1, \Lambda_2, \Lambda_3 \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \text{Gumbel}(0, 1)\),

  • Define \(c \, \colon = \sum_{s \in \mathcal S} \frac{\tilde\pi(s)^2}{\mu(s)}\),

\[ \textbf{Ansatz: } \, \mathcal P_n(u, v) \xrightarrow[n \to \infty]{p} \frac{1}{\lambda_B + 1}, \]

With this in mind, let \(\tilde \varepsilon > 0\). To prove the Ansatz, we want to bound the probability below from above as \(n \to \infty\).

\[ \begin{align*} \mathrm{P} \left( \Big| \mathcal P_n(u, v) - \frac{1}{\lambda_B + 1} \Big| \leq \tilde \varepsilon \right) & = \mathrm{P} \left( \frac{1}{\lambda_B + 1} - \tilde \varepsilon \leq \mathcal P_n(u, v) \leq \frac{1}{\lambda_B + 1} + \tilde \varepsilon \right), \\ \end{align*} \tag{7}\]

To this end,

  • let \(\varepsilon > 0\).

  • let \(L, U \in \mathbf R\) be such that \(\mathrm P (L \leq \Lambda_1 + \Lambda_2 + \Lambda_3 - c \leq U) \geq 1 - \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\),

  • let \(N \in \mathbf N\) be such that \(n \in \mathbf N_{\geq N} \implies\).

    • \(\Bigg| \mathrm P \left( L \leq \tilde \lambda_n \mathcal P_n(u,v) - \ln n \leq U\right) - \mathrm P (L \leq \Lambda_1 + \Lambda_2 + \Lambda_3 - c \leq U) \Bigg| \leq \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\) ((Kolossváry and Komjáthy 2015)’s Theorem 1.2. guarantees the existence of such an \(N\)),

    • \(\left| \frac{C_i}{(\lambda_B + 1) \cdot \ln N} \right| \leq \tilde \varepsilon \, \forall i \in \{1, 2\} \text{ for }C_1 = U, C_2 = L\).

We then have, by the triangle inequality, that

\[ \begin{align*} & \mathrm P \left( L \leq \tilde \lambda_n \mathcal P_n(u,v) - \ln n \leq U\right) + \Bigg| \mathrm P (L \leq \Lambda_1 + \Lambda_2 + \Lambda_3 - c \leq U) - \mathrm P \left( L \leq \tilde \lambda_n \mathcal P_n(u,v) - \ln n \leq U\right) \Bigg| \\ & \geq \mathrm P (L \leq \Lambda_1 + \Lambda_2 + \Lambda_3 - c \leq U) \\ & \geq 1 - \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \\ & \implies \\ & \mathrm P \left( L \leq \tilde \lambda_n \mathcal P_n(u,v) - \ln n \leq U\right) \\ &\geq 1 - \frac{\varepsilon}{2} - \Bigg| \mathrm P (L \leq \Lambda_1 + \Lambda_2 + \Lambda_3 - c \leq U) - \mathrm P \left( L \leq \tilde \lambda_n \mathcal P_n(u,v) - \ln n \leq U\right) \Bigg| \\ & \geq 1 - \varepsilon. \end{align*} \] \[ \mathrm P \left( L \leq \tilde \lambda_n \mathcal P_n(u,v) - \ln n \leq U\right) \geq 1 - \varepsilon \quad \implies \quad \mathrm P \left( \frac{L + \ln n}{\tilde \lambda_n} \leq \mathcal P_n(u,v) \leq \frac{U + \ln n}{\tilde \lambda_n} \right) \geq 1 - \varepsilon. \tag{8}\]

By (Equation 4),

\[ \tilde \lambda_n = (\lambda_B + 1) \cdot \ln n, \]

were \(\lambda_B\) is the Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue of the matrix \(B\).

Note that

\[ \begin{align*} \frac{L + \ln n}{\tilde \lambda_n} & = \frac{L + \ln n}{(\lambda_B + 1) \cdot \ln n} \\ & = \frac{\ln n}{\ln n} \cdot \frac{\frac{L}{\ln n} + 1}{\lambda_B + 1} \\ & = \frac{\frac{L}{\ln n} + 1}{\lambda_B + 1} \\ & = \frac{L}{(\lambda_B + 1) \cdot \ln n} + \frac{1}{\lambda_B + 1} \\ & \geq \frac{1}{\lambda_B + 1} - \tilde \varepsilon\\ \end{align*} \tag{9}\]

Similarly, we have

\[ \frac{U + \ln n}{\tilde \lambda_n} \leq \frac{1}{\lambda_B + 1} + \tilde \varepsilon. \tag{10}\]

Looking back to (Equation 7), we then have

\[ \begin{align*} \mathrm{P} \left( \frac{1}{\lambda_B + 1} - \tilde \varepsilon \leq \mathcal P_n(u, v) \leq \frac{1}{\lambda_B + 1} + \tilde \varepsilon \right) & \geq \mathrm P \left( \frac{L + \ln n}{\tilde \lambda_n} \leq \mathcal P_n(u,v) \leq \frac{U + \ln n}{\tilde \lambda_n} \right) \\ & \geq 1 - \varepsilon. \end{align*} \]

Were the first inequality is a consequence of (Equation 9) and (Equation 10), while the second inequality is a consequence of (Equation 8).

This being true for all \(\varepsilon > 0\), we indeed have

\[ \mathrm{P} \left( \Big| \mathcal P_n(u, v) - \frac{1}{\lambda_B + 1} \Big| \leq \tilde \varepsilon \right) \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} 1. \]

This concludes the proof that

\[ \mathcal P_n(u, v) \xrightarrow[n \to \infty]{p} \frac{1}{\lambda_B + 1}. \]

Below, we assume the framework of finite weighted stochastic blockmodels (WSBM).

(Dreveton et al. 2024) shows that, among other things, under the assumption that \[ \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{\log n} {n \cdot p_{ab}^n} = 0, \]

the ratio

\(\frac{p_{ab}^{mb}}{p_{ab}}\) scales as \(\Theta\left(\frac{\log n}{n \rho_n} \right)\).

If one could loosen the requirement so that we only needed

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{\log n} {n \cdot p_{ab}^n} \leq c \in \mathbf R_{>0}, \] then we could choose \(p_{ab}^n = \frac{\log n}{n}\) so that

\(\frac{p_{ab}^{mb}}{p_{ab}}\) scales as \(\Theta\left(\frac{\log n}{n \rho_n} \right) = \Theta(1)\).

We will see later that this idea corresponds to the theorem we hope to prove in the dense setting where the average degree \(\tilde \lambda_n + 1 = \log n \to \infty\).

Definition: μ

Let \(\mathcal S\) be the set of possible types and let \(\mu\) be a Borel probability measure on \(\mathcal S\). We will use \(\mu\) to define the type distribution of the vertices in our graph.

\[ \begin{align*} & \text{Empirical distribution } \nu \text{ converges in probability to } \mu \iff \\ & \nu(S) = \frac{|\{i \in [n]: x_i \in S \}|}{n} \xrightarrow[n \to \infty]{p} \mu(S) \qquad \forall \mu\text{-continuity sets }S \subset \mathcal S. \\ & \\ & \\ & \text{In particular, for } S = \{t\}, \text{ we have:} \\ & \\ & \frac{|\{i \in [n]: \text{type}(x_i) = t \}|}{n} = \frac{n_t(n)}{n} = \frac{n_t}{n} \xrightarrow[n \to \infty]{p} \mu(\{t\}) = \mu_t. \end{align*} \]
Definition: G(n, κ)

  • Sample vertices \(x_n\),

  • \(\forall i, j \in \{1, \dots, n \} = [n], \text{ the edge } \{i, j\}\) is present in the inhomogeneous random graph (IHRG) \(G(n, \kappa)\) with probability \(p_{ij} = \frac{\kappa(x_i, x_j)}{n}\) independently of other edges,

  • Conditioned on the edges of \(G(n, \kappa)\), assign i.i.d. weights \(X_e \sim \text{Exp}(1)\) to each edge \(e\).

Definition: μ-continuity set

Let \(S \in \mathcal B ( \mathcal S)\). As \(S\) is a Borel set, it is measurable with respect to our vertex space \((\mathcal S, \mu, (x_n)_n)\). We say that \(S\) is \(\mu\)-continuous if \(\mu(\partial S) = 0\).

\(\mu\)-continuity is the key to expressing the condition on \(\kappa\) that guarantees the uniqueness of the giant component. More precisely, \(\kappa\) must quasi-irreducible. As the name suggests, this property is closely related to the notion of irreducibility for kernels.

Definition: irreducible kernel

\(\kappa\) is irreducible if \[ A \subset \mathcal S \quad \& \quad \kappa \equiv 0 \text{ on } A \times (\mathcal S \setminus A) \implies \mu(A) = 0 \quad \text{ or } \quad\mu(\mathcal S \setminus A) = 0. \]

The fact that \(\kappa \equiv 0\) on \(A \times (\mathcal S \setminus A)\) teaches us that:

Starting from \(A\), one cannot exit \(A\). In this sense, \(A\) is isolated from the rest of the graph. Thus, the emergence of components in \(A\) is completely independent from the emergence of components in \(\mathcal S \setminus A\).

To guarantee uniqueness of the giant component in the super-critical regime, we want to avoid this. \(\mu(A) = 0\) or \(\mu(\mathcal S \setminus A) = 0\) does exactly that as it ensures asymptotically that the vertices will land in positions, from which there is a strictly positive probability of reaching any other vertex.

Definition: quasi-irreducible kernel

\(\kappa\) is quasi-irreducible if \(\exists\) a \(\mu\)-continuous set \(\mathcal S' \subset \mathcal S\) with \(\mu(\mathcal S') > 0\) s.t. \(\kappa(x, y) = 0 \text{ if } x \notin \mathcal S'\) or \(y \notin \mathcal S'\).

In essence, this allows us to ignore the vertices in \(\mathcal S \setminus \mathcal S'\) and start over with a new ground space \((\mathcal S', \mu')\), where \(\mu' = \frac{\mu}{\mu(\mathcal S')}\) on which our kernel is irreducible.

The fact that \(\mu(\partial \mathcal S') = 0\) ensures that the separation is « clean » and that one can serenely ignore the vertices in \(\mathcal S \setminus \mathcal S'\) as explained above.

Definition: graphical kernel

A sequence of kernels \((\kappa_n)\) on a vertex space \((\mathcal{S}, \mu, (x_n))\) is graphical with limit \(\boldsymbol{\kappa} \in L^1(\mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{S}, \mu \times \mu) \text{ and continuous a.e.}\) if - For a.e. \((y, z) \in \mathcal{S}^2\): \(y_n \to y\) & \(z_n \to z \implies \kappa(y_n, z_n) \to \kappa(y, z),\)

  • \(\frac{1}{n} E_{\mu}[e(G(n, \kappa))] \to \int_{\mathcal{S}^2} \kappa(x, y) \text{ } d \mu(x) \text{ } d \mu(y).\)
Motivation \[ \begin{align*} \frac{1}{2} \int_{\mathcal S^2} \kappa(x, y) \mathop{}\!\mathrm{d} \mu(x) \mathop{}\!\mathrm{d} \mu(y) & = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{xy} \kappa(x, y) \mu(x) \mu(y) \\ & = \frac{1}{2} \sum_x \mu(x) \sum_y \kappa(x, y) \mu(y) \\ & \approx \frac{1}{2} \sum_x \frac{n_x}{n} \sum_y \kappa(x, y) \mu(y)\\ & = \frac{1}{n} \cdot \frac{1}{2} \sum_x n_x \sum_y \kappa(x, y) \mu(y)\\ & = \frac{1}{n} \cdot \frac{1}{2} \sum_x n_x \mathrm{E} \left[ \text{deg}(v_x) \right] \\ & = \frac{1}{n} \mathrm{E} \left[ e(G(n, \kappa)) | v_1, \dots v_n\right] \end{align*} \] \[ \implies \frac{1}{2} \int_{\mathcal S^2} \kappa(x, y) \mathop{}\!\mathrm{d} \mu(x) \mathop{}\!\mathrm{d} \mu(y) = \mathrm{E} \left[ \frac{1}{2} \int_{\mathcal S^2} \kappa(x, y) \mathop{}\!\mathrm{d} \mu(x) \mathop{}\!\mathrm{d} \mu(y) \right] = \frac{1}{n} \mathrm{E} \left[ \mathrm{E} \left[ e(G(n, \kappa)) | v_1, \dots v_n\right] \right] = \frac{1}{n} \mathrm{E} \left[ e(G(n, \kappa)) \right]. \]
Definition: first passage percolation

First passage percolation (FPP) is a stochastic model introduced to study random growth and flow processes on graphs.

This article shows turns the problem of finding the shortest path (weighted or not) between two vertices \(u\) and \(v\), into finding the path associated to the first passage time from \(u\) to \(v\). That is the path through which the minimum amount of time is needed for the liquid to get from \(u\) to \(v\).

Example 1: the Erdös-Rényi random graph

Let \(\mathcal S = \{1\}\).

\(G(n, p) \sim G(n, \kappa)\) for \(\kappa \equiv c\), where \(c\) is a strictly positive constant s.t. \(c = p \cdot n\).

Indeed, the edge probabilities are then given by

\[ p_{ij} = \frac{\kappa(v_i, v_j)}{n} = \frac{c}{n} = p. \]

Also note that \(\| T_{\kappa} \| = c\) since the constant function \(\mathbf 1\) is an eigenvector of \(T_{\kappa}\) associated to the eigenvalue \(c\).

Example 2: the homogeneous bipartite random graph

Let us show how \(G(m, m, p) \sim G(n, \kappa)\) for \(p = \frac{c}{m}\) and \(m = \frac{n}{2}\).

There are two types of vertices \(\implies\) we can define the type space as

\[ \mathcal S \, \colon = \{1, 2\}. \]

Define the Borel probability measure \(\mu\) on \(\mathcal B( \mathcal S)\) by

\[ \mu(\{1\}) = \mu(\{2\}) = \frac{1}{2}. \]

The kernel \(\kappa\) is then defined by

\[ \kappa(1, 1) = \kappa(2, 2) = 0 \quad \& \quad \kappa(1, 2) = \kappa(2, 1) = 2c. \]

Finally, we decide only to define \(x_n\) only for even \(n\) and set \(m \, \colon = \frac{n}{2}\). Also, \((x_n)\) is a deterministic sample with \(m\) vertices of type 1 and \(m\) vertices of type 2.

The edge probabilities are then given by

\[ p_{ij} = \frac{2c}{n} = \frac{c}{m} = p \quad \forall i, j \in [n] \text{ s.t. } x_i \neq x_j, \] so that we have the desired homogeneous bipartite graph \(G(m, m, p)\).

Example 3: the homogeneous case
Suppose \[ \int_\mathcal S \kappa(s, t) \mathop{}\!\mathrm{d} \mu(t) = c \quad \forall s \in \mathcal S. \]

Intuitively, this means that asymptotically, the average vertex degree is equal to \(c\) and is independent of the vertex type.

Note that

\[ c \cdot \mathbf 1(s) = c = \int_\mathcal S \kappa(s, t) \mathop{}\!\mathrm{d} \mu(t) = \int_\mathcal S \kappa(s, t) \mathbf 1(t) \mathop{}\!\mathrm{d} \mu(t) = T_{\kappa} \mathbf 1(s). \]

so that the constant function \(\mathbf 1\) is an eigenvector of \(T_{\kappa}\) associated to the eigenvalue \(c\). By the Min-Max theorem for compact, self-adjoint operators, we have that \(\| T_{\kappa} \| = c\).

This allows us to ignore the type-specific details when studying the giant component.

Indeed, just like in the Erdös-Rényi case, the emergence of the giant component depends only whether \(c > 1\) or not.

Step 0: Start with the finite-type case.

Step 1: frame the problem of finding the shortest path between two vertices \(u\) and \(v\) as a first passage percolation problem.

To find the shortest-weight path between two vertices x and y, we consider the edge weights as lengths and let fluid percolate through the edges of the graph simultaneously at a constant rate, starting from both vertices. We call this procedure the exploration processes of the neighborhoods of vertices x and y.

Step 2: relate the exploration processes to a branching process.

Define a continuous-time branching process (CTBP) \(\Psi_{\kappa}^s\) with

  • root of type \(s\),
  • If root is of type \(s\), the number of root’s children of type \(t\) is \(\text{Poisson}(\kappa(s, t) \mu_t)\).
  • Waiting times are \(\text{Exp}(1)\).

Intuitively, when the flows collide, the shortest-weight path has been found: up to any time τ each exploration process contains the vertices with distance at most τ from x or y. It is standard to relate an exploration process to a branching process (BP). In our context this BP is a continuous-time multitype BP, which we introduce in Section 2. We can apply BP theory once we embed the BP into the IHRG: in Section 3 we assign labels to the vertices of the BP according to their type in a way that we can rule out loop and multiple edges in the embedding in the IHRG. We thin the BP to deal with cycles. The problem with the scenario of colliding flows in continuous time is that we can keep track of the flows only in discrete steps as new vertices are explored. So we first start the exploration process from x until a given number of vertices are explored and stop. Then we start the flow from y and wait until a vertex vy which shares an edge with a wet vertex vx in the flow of x becomes wet. We have then just found a path between x and y through vx and vy, which, however, might not be the shortest one. Thus, we have to minimize over all such possible choices of vx and vy . The rigorous treatment of the connection time is contained in Section 4. Finally, in Section 5 we prove our main results, Theorems 1.1 and 1.2.

Properties:

  • Let \(\omega, \eta \in \{0, 1\}^E\) be two configurations. We define the following ordering:

\(\qquad \qquad \omega \leq \eta \quad \iff \quad \omega(e) \leq \eta(e) \quad \forall e \in E.\)

  • An event \(A \in \mathcal{F}\) is increasing if

    \(\qquad \omega \in A \quad \& \quad \omega \leq \eta \implies \eta \in A.\)

    • Examples: \(\{x \xleftrightarrow{} y\} \text{ } \& \text{ } \{|C_x| \geq 10\}\) are increasing events.

    • Remark : \(A, B\) increasing \(\implies A \cap B \text{ } \& \text{ } A \cup B\) increasing.

    • Nonexample: \(\{|C_0| = 10\}\) is not increasing.

  • A function \(f: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}\) is increasing if

    \(\qquad \omega \leq \eta \implies f(\omega) \leq f(\eta).\)

    • Example: \(f(\omega) = |C_0(\omega)|\) is an increasing function.

    • Connection: event \(A\) increasing \(\iff\) function \(\mathbb{1}_A\) increasing.

  • Les \((\Omega, \mathcal{A}, P)\) be a probability space.
    A map \(X: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}\) is a random variable if \(X^{-1}(B) \stackrel{\text{notation}}{=} \{X \in B\} \in \mathcal{A}\) for all Borel sets \(B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\).
    In other words: \(X\) must a measurable map from \((\Omega, \mathcal{A})\) to \((\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))\). Note that the probability measure is irrelevant here.

    Each random variable induces a probability measure on \(\mathbb{R}\) by \(P_X(B) = P(X \in B) = P(X^{-1}(B))\) called the law of \(X\).

  • Proposition:
    1. Let \(A \in \mathcal{F}\) be an increasing event, then
      \(\qquad p \mapsto \mathbb{P}_p(A)\) is non-decreasing.
